Step one · tokens

Models see tokens, not words!

Natural Language Processing (NLP) starts by chopping your input into tokens: sub-word chunks mapped to integer IDs. The model reads those IDs, never the raw text.
0 tokens 0 characters 0 chars per token
TD Builders Club S06 / 08 · Tokenizer From your COO interns
Type anything into the box. Watch how common words become single tokens and rare or long words split into pieces. Tokens are why API costs are measured in tokens, and why very long inputs get expensive.
Step two · weights and context

Weights, parameters, context window.

Three words people mix up. They describe different axes of the same machine.
X · parameters Y · layers Z · context
Parameters
The dials. Billions of them.
Numbers the model adjusts during training. A 7B model has 7 billion of these. More parameters usually means more capacity, more compute, and a bigger download.
Weights
The values in the dials.
A snapshot of parameter values at a point in time. When people say open weights they mean the trained numbers are downloadable.
Context window
How much it can hold in mind.
The number of tokens the model can look at in one pass. Older models had 2k. Frontier models now handle 200k and up. Bigger context means longer inputs but more cost per call.
TD Builders Club S06 / 08 · Weights and context From your COO interns
Parameters is the count, weights is the values, context is the working memory. Different axes, different tradeoffs.
Step three · how it learns

Rolling downhill on a loss surface.

Every model is just numbers trying to minimize error. Training walks those numbers in the direction that lowers the loss the fastest.

The idea

L(θ)  =  loss on data given weights θ
θt+1  =  θt − η ∇L(θt)

∇L is the gradient. It points uphill. Subtracting it steps downhill. η is how big the step is.

What you are seeing

The surface on the right is a loss landscape. Drop a ball anywhere on it. It follows the steepest path down until it settles in a valley. That valley is a set of weights that make the model less wrong.

Click any of the four drop points. The ball rolls downhill on the surface z = sin(x)cos(y) + 0.2(x^2 + y^2)*0.1. Different starts, different valleys. That is why training runs with different seeds can land on different solutions.
Step four · prediction machines

A language model guesses the next token. Then again. Then again.

A Markov chain is the simplest version. Trained on the text you just tokenized, it learns which words tend to follow which. It picks the next token, appends it, and plays the game again.

Step five · retrieval

M365 Copilot is mostly RAG.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation. Before the model answers, a search step pulls in the documents, emails, or files that look relevant. The model reads those and writes a reply grounded in them.
Retrieval
Augmented
Generation

Why this matters

  • Your data stays put. The base model does not need to be retrained on internal docs. The search layer just fetches what is relevant at query time.
  • Answers are traceable. Because the model quotes retrieved chunks, Copilot can cite the file it used and you can verify it.
  • Access rules are respected. If a document is not shared with you, retrieval will not surface it. Permissions carry through.
  • Freshness is cheap. Update a document, re-index it, and the next answer already reflects the change. No fine-tune, no redeploy.
TD Builders Club S06 / 08 · RAG From your COO interns
Copilot is not one big magic model. It is a smaller pipeline: search over your tenant, then a generation step. The interesting engineering is mostly in the search.
